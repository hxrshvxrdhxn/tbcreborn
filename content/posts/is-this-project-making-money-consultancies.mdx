In engineering consultancies, design firms and advisory practices, the most expensive sentence is "we'll know at the end". The fee is fixed, the scope moves, the hours pile up, and the margin is only clear when the final invoice goes out.

By then the lessons are useful for the next project but no help to this one.

## Why margins slip quietly

Project margins rarely collapse in one go. They wear away through a handful of familiar habits:

- **Timesheets are filled in at the end of the week, or the month,** so effort is always reported late.
- **Progress is reported as a feeling.** "Mostly done" is not a percentage you can compare with hours spent.
- **Small client requests are absorbed.** Each one is reasonable on its own, and none of them is recorded as extra scope.
- **The weekly client report takes half a day,** so it gets written from memory rather than from the project data.

## The one comparison that matters

For each project, you want to see two numbers side by side, every week:

1. **Fee earned:** how much of the fee the completed deliverables represent.
2. **Effort spent:** how much of the budgeted hours have been used.

If 40% of the fee is earned and 70% of the hours are gone, you have a problem you can still do something about. You can rescope, renegotiate, change the team, or at least warn the partner before the client meeting rather than after.

## Track client comments to closure

A large share of overruns comes from comments and requests that were never logged. A simple rule helps: every client comment becomes a tracked item with an owner and a status. When a request goes beyond the agreed scope, it is flagged as a variation, and either priced or consciously absorbed. Either decision is fine. An unconscious one is not.

## Let the report write itself

The weekly progress report should come from the week's data: deliverables completed, hours logged, comments open and closed, and the next milestone. If those are recorded as the work happens, drafting the report takes minutes, and the partner's time goes on checking it rather than assembling it.
