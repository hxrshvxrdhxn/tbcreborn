In most small and mid-sized food, nutraceutical, pharma and specialty chemical plants, the certificate of analysis is the last document a customer sees and the first one an auditor asks for. It is also, very often, retyped by hand.

The QC chemist records results in a register or a spreadsheet. Someone in the office opens a Word template, copies the values across, updates the batch number and dates, and sends a PDF. It works most of the time. The problem is the rest of the time.

## Where the errors come from

A wrong digit on a COA rarely comes from bad testing. It comes from the copy:

- A value from the previous batch left in the template.
- A decimal point moved when typing 0.05 as 0.5.
- The right result against the wrong specification limit.
- A batch released before one test was complete, because the COA looked finished.

Each of these is a small slip. Together they are a real risk: a customer complaint, a rejected consignment, or an audit observation that asks how you control the link between your test data and the certificate you issue.

## The audit question you need to answer

Auditors, whether for FSSAI, GMP, ISO or a large customer's vendor audit, want to see one thing clearly: that the number on the certificate is the number from the test, and that a batch outside specification cannot be released by accident.

If your honest answer is "we check it carefully", that is a people control. It depends on the same person being careful on the busiest day of the month.

## A better way to run batch records and QC

The plants that handle this well tend to follow a few simple principles:

1. **Record each result once, at source.** The chemist enters the result against the batch and the specification. That entry is the record.
2. **Judge results against the spec automatically.** Pass, fail or out of specification is worked out by the system, not by reading across a table.
3. **Hold the batch when a result fails.** An out-of-specification result puts the batch on hold until QA reviews it. Release is a deliberate step, signed by a named person.
4. **Build the COA from the recorded data.** The certificate is generated from what was recorded, so there is nothing to retype and nothing to drift.
5. **Make it traceable both ways.** From a raw material lot to every batch that used it and every customer who received it, and back again.

## Traceability is the part people forget

When a customer raises a complaint about one raw material lot, the questions come fast: which batches used it, which customers got those batches, and what the test results were. If that answer takes a day of searching registers, you are on the back foot. If it takes a minute, you are in control of the conversation.
