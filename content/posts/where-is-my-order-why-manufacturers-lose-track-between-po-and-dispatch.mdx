Most order-to-dispatch problems in a growing factory do not start on the shop floor. They start in the gap between departments. Sales takes the purchase order, planning puts it on a sheet, purchase chases the material, production works from a job card, and dispatch finds out about it when the truck is booked. Everyone does their part. Nobody can see the whole order.

The symptom is familiar: the customer calls and asks where the order is, and someone has to walk the floor or ring three people to find out.

## The real cost of "let me check and call you back"

That call is not just an interruption. It tells you three things about how the business runs:

- **Status lives in people's heads.** The answer exists, but only the person who handled the last step knows it.
- **Delays are found late.** If material arrives two days late, the order usually looks fine until the dispatch date is already at risk.
- **The customer finds out before you do.** By the time they ask, they have often planned around a date you can no longer meet.

In a factory doing fifty orders a month, this is manageable with a good planner and a spreadsheet. At two hundred orders across several product lines, the spreadsheet stops being the truth. Different versions circulate on WhatsApp and email, and the weekly review meeting becomes the only time anyone sees the full picture.

## What a working order board looks like

The fix is not more reporting. It is one place where every order is visible from the purchase order to dispatch, with a few simple rules:

1. **Every order has stages, and every stage has an owner.** Material, machining, assembly, inspection, packing, dispatch. Each stage has one named person responsible for moving it on.
2. **Each stage has a due date that links to the next.** If material is late, machining, assembly and dispatch move with it automatically, so the risk shows up the day the delay happens, not the day before dispatch.
3. **Updates come from the floor, not from meetings.** A supervisor scans a job card or sends a short voice note, and the order moves. Nobody types the same status into three places.
4. **Customers get updates when you choose to send them.** A clear message ("packed, dispatching tomorrow at 10 am") stops the chase calls before they start.

## Early warning is the whole point

The most useful thing a board like this does is turn an order amber before it turns red. When a stage slips, the orders affected by it are flagged, and the owner can act while there is still time: expedite material, move a job to another machine, or call the customer with an honest revised date.

That is the difference between managing delays and explaining them.

## Where to start

You do not need to change your ERP or your accounts package to get this. Start with three things:

- List the stages every order goes through, in your own words.
- Name one owner per stage.
- Decide which updates the customer should get, and when.

Once those are clear, the tool is the easy part.
